# Objection response patterns

Use the smallest pattern that fits. The examples illustrate structure, not facts to repeat.

## Missing information or misunderstanding

1. Restate the point neutrally.
2. Ask what interpretation or evidence the buyer is using.
3. Correct only what the seller can substantiate.
4. Offer the authoritative source or name the open question.

Pattern: “I may not have explained that clearly. Which part is creating the concern? What we can support today is [source-backed point]; [open item] still needs confirmation.”

## Product or fit gap

Do not turn a missing capability into a roadmap promise. Establish whether the requirement is essential, whether a current supported alternative exists, and whether the alternative introduces material cost or risk. If the gap is disqualifying, say so.

Pattern: “We do not support [requirement] today. If that is mandatory, this may not be the right fit. If [supported alternative] is acceptable, we can evaluate it against your constraint.”

## Trust, implementation, security, or operational risk

Ask what failure the buyer needs to prevent and what evidence would be sufficient. Use only current, authorized documentation. Security, privacy, legal, or compliance answers should be reviewed by the responsible owner when they exceed available authority.

Pattern: “The risk you are trying to avoid is [reported risk]. We can substantiate [evidence]. I cannot confirm [open issue] without [responsible owner/source].”

## Timing or changed priority

Distinguish an externally fixed date, a competing internal priority, and a polite decline. Do not invent a cost of delay or deadline. Offer a low-pressure revisit only if the buyer has not asked for no further contact.

## Soft refusal

Make it easy to decline. A useful check is: “Would you prefer to close this out, revisit after a specific condition changes, or answer one unresolved question first?” Respect the choice.

## Commercial negotiation

Clarify the value or risk issue first. If the user then wants to design a numeric or contractual exchange, use `sales-negotiation` when available. Do not discount merely to make discomfort disappear.

## Legal or procurement constraint

Separate a negotiable business preference from language requiring legal, security, finance, or procurement approval. Draft questions and options; do not provide final legal conclusions or imply acceptance.
